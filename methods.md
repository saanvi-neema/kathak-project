# Methods

This project has multiple separate pilot experiments (see project_description.md for the full list). Each section below documents what was actually done for one experiment, in plain language, so it's clear later what was tested and how.

## The Vertical Stack: How the Full Pipeline Fits Together

The end goal: upload one performance video, get back a dashboard app with a detailed, timestamped critique — e.g. *"At 0:42, your chakkar did not return to your starting orientation, you did about 3.75 spins instead of 4. At 1:10, your movements were not on beat. You ended the piece about 1 second before the ending beat. At 1:35, your tatkaar was off-beat."* The deliverable format was undecided for a long time; it's now settled as an app (a dashboard mockup was used as the target reference), not a paper.

### Processing pipeline

1. **Extract landmarks.** MediaPipe finds body and hand joint positions in every frame. **Built, working.**

2. **Extract features.** Turn raw joint positions into actual measurements: joint angles, torso tilt, left/right symmetry, velocity/acceleration (body); finger curl, thumb-to-fingertip distances, finger spread (hands); foot position/trajectory (feet). **Body and hands built. Footwork built but never tested on real tatkaar footage — none has been recorded yet.**

3. **Chakkar analysis.** Count rotations from the shoulder-angle signal. **Built.** Counting is proven — exact match on 1/3/10-spin test clips, no drift even at speed. Raw count rounds to the nearest clean landing and reports the gap (no external answer key needed — chakkar sequences are choreographed to land clean, so a messy number like 3.75 is itself evidence of an error); ending shoulder-angle is checked against where the dancer started (see the real wording bug below — not "facing front"); the stop is checked for a controlled slowdown vs. an abrupt cutoff, to confirm a flagged gap is a real error and not measurement noise.

   **Real bug found and fixed: "ending orientation" was mislabeled as "facing front."** Caught in an external review, then verified directly against the code: `chakkar_scoring.py`'s orientation check has never had any external reference for where "front" actually is (no stage markers, no camera-facing calibration) -- it can only measure whether the dancer returned to wherever THEY were facing at the *start* of that chakkar (`start_ref`, the mean of the first ~10 frames). The code comment already said this honestly, but the user-facing flag text didn't -- it said "your chakkar did not end facing front," which overstates what was actually measured (finishing near the camera vs. finishing near your own starting position are different claims). Fixed by rewording every user-facing instance (`report_assembly.flags_from_chakkar()`, `chakkar_scoring.describe()`, this doc's own target example) to "did not return to your starting orientation," and renaming the internal `front_ref` variable to `start_ref` so the code doesn't quietly reintroduce the same confusion later. The dashboard's own label ("Ending orientation gap") was already neutral and didn't need changing.

   **Multi-event segmentation, also built.** The above scores a rotation event -- it originally scored the whole clip as one event, which is only correct when the whole clip is one continuous spin (true for the 3 ground-truth pilot clips, false for a real piece). Confirmed directly on movement_01.mov (a real Birju Maharaj piece excerpt with arm movement, chakkar, and footwork mixed together): scoring it as one clip gave a falsely "clean" blended result (raw_count=2.01, quality=100, zero flags) that hid several separate rotation bursts, none of which were individually clean landings. Fixed by segmenting first: a sliding window computes net rotation rate (deg/sec), and a sustained stretch above a speed threshold is treated as one candidate chakkar burst. The threshold was calibrated directly against movement_01.mov's real data, not guessed — genuine chakkar bursts measured 150-400+ deg/s in every 0.5s window they covered, while ordinary arm-driven shoulder movement stayed under ~100 deg/s except right at a burst's edge. One more real finding from that calibration: a single isolated window reading as "spinning" turned out to be a one-frame tracking glitch (a momentary shoulder-tracking swap), not a real rotation — filtered out by requiring a minimum sustained duration, not just one window over threshold. Each detected burst is then scored independently (its own count/orientation/stop-quality, not the whole clip's), and only bursts covering at least half a turn count as a real chakkar. Validated both directions: the 3 ground-truth clips still segment as exactly one event each with unchanged results, and movement_01.mov correctly segments into multiple candidate bursts instead of one blended number (tests/test_chakkar_scoring.py).

   **Drift-during-spin, also built, deliberately unscored.** Prompted by a direct question: spinning with clean rotation/orientation/stop-quality can still involve traveling a real distance across the floor -- not caught by anything above. `chakkar_scoring.compute_drift()` reports how far the center of mass moved during a chakkar segment, normalized by shoulder width (a body-scale unit, since raw pixel distance is meaningless without knowing camera distance, and there's no camera calibration here for real-world units like feet) -- plus a path-straightness ratio (net distance moved ÷ total distance covered; 1.0 = a straight line, lower = more back-and-forth). This is intentionally **not** turned into a flag or score penalty: a traveling chakkar (deliberately covering ground while spinning) is a real, intentional choreographic element in Kathak, not necessarily a mistake, and there's no way to tell intent from video alone -- only the dancer/teacher knows whether a given move was supposed to travel. Reported as plain numbers in the Chakkar Analysis tab for reference. **Honest caveat found while testing against real footage**: even a normal-looking chakkar in movement_01.mov measured a fairly low straightness (0.34), suggesting some path "wobble" is a normal byproduct of spinning itself (the centroid of shoulders+hips isn't perfectly still even during clean in-place rotation), not necessarily a sign of instability -- so this ratio is weaker evidence of "erratic vs. controlled" than hoped, which is exactly why it stays unscored rather than being turned into a judgment. No validated threshold exists yet for what drift amount or straightness would actually indicate a problem; that would need real footage of both traveling and unintentional-drift chakkars to calibrate against, which doesn't exist yet.

4. **Mudra analysis.** Compare the hand geometry measured in step 2 against a reference definition of what each mudra should look like (e.g. "in Pataka, thumb tip should be near the palm, other four fingers straight and together"), and flag mismatches like *"your thumb was not connected to your palm as it should be in Pataka."* **Built**, in two parts:

   **Part A: rule-checking a NAMED mudra.** `mudra_reference.py` has geometric rules for all 28 asamyukta hastas (extended/curled fingers, thumb-touch distance, finger spread), `check_mudra()`/`check_mudra_variants()` compare a hand's measured features against a given mudra's rule and return plain-English mismatches. This part has existed since early in the project.

   **Part B: identifying WHICH mudra is being held, then checking it.** This was the genuinely stuck piece -- it needed labeled training data, and the original plan (scrub one long recorded clip by hand for ground-truth timestamps) never worked cleanly. Solved two ways:
   - **Training data**: instead of recording it, found `jisharajr/Bharatanatyam-Mudra-Dataset` (GitHub, CC-BY-SA 4.0) -- 15,396 real single-hand mudra photos from 15 volunteers. Bharatanatyam and Kathak's asamyukta hastas share the same Natya Shastra vocabulary, and 29 of this project's 30 mudra/variant labels (27 base mudras + katakamukh's 3 variants, missing only "sandansh") matched the dataset's folders exactly, cross-checked by hand against the README, not fuzzy-matched. `build_mudra_training_data_from_images.py` runs MediaPipe hand detection (IMAGE mode) over 150 photos per class (capped for runtime, not scarcity -- each folder has 450-600+ available) and produces a labeled dataset in the same schema `build_mudra_training_data.py`'s video-clip approach would have produced, so `mudra_classifier.py` doesn't care which one built it. **Real finding while running this**: MediaPipe's default hand-detection confidence (0.5, tuned for video) only found a hand in ~45% of these studio photos (tight crops, unusual angles) -- lowered to 0.3 after checking directly that this didn't produce degenerate detections, recovering ~60%.
   - **Classifier**: `mudra_classifier.py` reuses `extract_features.py`'s existing hand-geometry features (finger curl, thumb-to-fingertip distances, spread) and trains a RandomForestClassifier. On the real 4,350-image dataset (150 images x 29 labels): **86% held-out accuracy**. Confusions were concentrated exactly where expected -- e.g. ardhapataka (tripataka + bent pinky) getting predicted as tripataka -- not random noise.
   - **Wired into `app/pipeline.py`**: `run_mudra_analysis()` finds stable (non-transitioning) hand-hold windows in an uploaded video the same way `build_mudra_training_data.py` finds reps within a training clip, classifies each hold, then runs that predicted mudra's own rule check on it -- combining identification with correctness-checking, the actual original target behavior. Falls back to `None` honestly if no trained model exists at `MUDRA_MODEL_PATH`.

   **Second real dataset added: `demon2angel/asanyukta-kathak-mudra` (Kaggle, CC BY 4.0)** -- 1,560 images, 26 classes, genuinely Kathak (not Bharatanatyam), found after checking a lead that had been noted but not followed up on earlier. YOLO-detection format (images/ + labels/ + data.yaml), not folders-per-class, so it needed its own extraction script (`build_mudra_training_data_from_kaggle.py`) reading the class index out of each label file. Combined with a full (uncapped) re-extraction of the Bharatanatyam set -- 12,476 images instead of the original 150/class cap, since each folder actually has 450-600+ available -- for **13,603 total labeled images**.

   **Real, rigorous accuracy testing against this project's own recorded footage (`data/landmarks/mudra_01.csv`), not just held-out accuracy on more photos:**
   - First pass: held-out accuracy on photos went up with more data (85% to 90%), but accuracy on the actual video frames it's meant to run on did *not* improve, and one previously-strong result got *worse*. That was the first sign the held-out number wasn't measuring the right thing -- photos and real video frames are different enough (posed vs. natural, studio-lit vs. real lighting) that piling on more photos doesn't close that gap by itself.
   - Went through all 24 detected hold-windows in `mudra_01.mov` by hand against frame images, then against the actual recorded performance order (28 mudras + katakamukh's 3 variants, performed once each) -- not guessed, the real order. Manual visual verification hit a real wall: several consecutive mudras in the sequence (tripataka/ardhapataka/kartarimukh; separately ardhachandra/padmakosh/sarpasheesh/hanspaksha/sandansh) differ only by exact thumb placement, which is genuinely hard to read from a phone-selfie-angle video, not a matter of looking more carefully.
   - **Found and fixed the actual root cause**, not another workaround: `mudra_reference.py`'s thumb-touch/finger-spread checks used a single absolute normalized-coordinate threshold (`TOUCH_THRESHOLD = 0.08`), which assumes the hand appears at the same scale in every video. It doesn't -- confirmed directly when the very first detected window in `mudra_01.mov` (which *must* be Pataka; it's the first thing recorded) failed Pataka's own thumb-touch check under the old threshold. Fixed two ways:
     1. `extract_features.py`'s thumb-to-fingertip and adjacent-finger-spread distances are now normalized by `hand_scale` (wrist-to-middle-MCP distance, the same "normalize by a stable body-scale reference" principle already used for chakkar drift) -- so a threshold means "close relative to this hand's own size," not a fixed number tied to one dataset's camera distance.
     2. A single "touching" threshold doesn't work even after normalizing -- thumb-to-pinky touching measures meaningfully closer than thumb-to-index touching, for real anatomical reasons (the thumb travels a shorter path to the pinky than to the index). Replaced with **per-finger thresholds** (`TOUCH_THRESHOLDS` dict in `mudra_reference.py`), calibrated from real median/p75 touching-vs-not-touching distances measured across the full 13,603-image training set, not guessed -- then nudged once more (index: 0.91 to 1.05) after checking against the real, guaranteed-correct Pataka frame, since real video has more natural variation than posed photos.
   - **Result after the fix, retrained on the corrected features**: re-ran the same 24 real hold-windows against the real recorded sequence. 13 of 24 now land on the exact correct mudra in the exact correct sequence position (tripataka, ardhapataka, ardhachandra, mushti, kapitth, katakamukh_1, soochi, chandrakala, mrigasheesh, singhamukh, alapadma, hanspaksha, trishool -- all confirmed against the real recording order). The remaining errors are now concentrated in *explainable* confusion between genuinely similar mudras (katakamukh_1/mukul/shikhar -- all "curled fist, thumb touches a finger"; chandrakala/tamrachud -- both "only index extended") rather than incoherent noise. This is the first real, trustworthy accuracy read this feature has had -- everything before it was either testing the wrong thing (held-out photo accuracy) or measuring against a broken threshold.

   **`MUDRA_MIN_CONFIDENCE` calibrated against the same 23 verified real windows**, not left as a guess. Correct predictions averaged confidence 0.44, wrong ones averaged 0.29 -- a real but not clean separation (correct as low as 0.23, wrong as high as 0.49, so no threshold cleanly splits them). The old default of 0.5 was far too conservative: it only let through 23% of correct predictions just to screen out nearly all the wrong ones. Moved to **0.30**, trading some precision (~71% of what passes is right, down from ~100%) for much better recall (~77% of correct predictions get shown, up from ~23%) -- more useful for a "here's what to check" tool than a handful of near-certain answers on an otherwise-empty tab.

   **Honest gaps that remain:**
   - Real accuracy on actual performance footage is genuinely in the 50-60% range right now, not the 85-90% the training-photo metric suggests. That's the number to trust -- **as of the model this was measured against.** `model.joblib` has since been retrained (a later audit fix to `predict_mudra()`'s imputation logic required a bundle key the old on-disk model predated -- it raised an error on every single prediction until the retrain, a real regression that briefly broke mudra identification in both live and upload flows). A spot-check of the retrained model against `mudra_01.mov` shows the same shape of result (24 detected hold-windows, confidence mean ~0.46, predicted labels following the same relative order as the previously hand-verified correct answers) -- nothing suggests a regression -- but the rigorous 13/24 hand-verification above has not been redone against the new model. Treat the 50-60% figure as pre-retrain history until that re-check happens.
   - The confidence calibration above is based on 23 data points from one video -- a real, directionally trustworthy signal, but not a large enough sample to call the exact threshold value final. Worth revisiting once more labeled real footage exists.
   - "sandansh" has no coverage in either external dataset (and is described as a repeated open/close *motion*, not a static shape, so a photo dataset structurally can't represent it well even if one existed).
   - Both external datasets' licenses (CC-BY-SA 4.0, CC BY 4.0) require attribution and carry forward to anything shared/published built from them. `data/mudra_training/` (raw external images, derived dataset CSVs, trained model) is gitignored entirely -- regenerable from the scripts, and too large/inappropriate to commit regardless.

   Validated with synthetic regression tests (`tests/test_mudra_classifier.py`, `tests/test_pipeline_mudra_integration.py`, `tests/test_build_mudra_training_data.py`) -- these pin the plumbing (held-window detection, feature extraction, training, prediction, rule-checking all correctly wired together), not real-world accuracy, which can only be checked against real data as above.

   **Real bug found and fixed: "Mudra Accuracy" was circular.** Caught in an external code review, then verified directly against the code, not taken on faith: `run_mudra_analysis()` predicts a mudra with the classifier, then checks that SAME predicted label's own geometric rule against the observed hand shape. So the old "Mudra Accuracy" score only ever answered "does the hand shape agree with whatever the classifier guessed" -- never "was the guess actually right." A confidently WRONG identification (e.g. tripataka misread as ardhapataka) could still score a clean 100%, because the rule check has no way to know the guess was wrong; it was only ever checking the prediction against itself. Fixed by separating the two questions instead of conflating them:
   - `score_aggregation.mudra_accuracy_score()` renamed to `mudra_rule_agreement_score()`, with its docstring stating plainly what it does and doesn't measure.
   - New `mudra_identification_accuracy_score()` -- a *real* accuracy score, but only computable when the user supplies what they actually intended to perform. The upload form has an optional "Expected mudra sequence" field (mirroring the taal/sam section's pattern: optional, collapsed, explicitly not auto-detected); each detected hold is matched positionally against the supplied sequence (`_attach_expected_mudras()`, a small pure function pulled out of `run_mudra_analysis()` specifically so this matching logic is unit-testable without needing real hand-motion data to produce multiple held windows).
   - `overall_score()` now prefers real identification accuracy over rule-agreement whenever an expected sequence was supplied, instead of silently blending the weaker signal in.
   - The dashboard shows both scores distinctly when both exist, with rule-agreement's caveat spelled out in the tab itself, and per-event "expected: X (correct/mismatch)" annotations when a sequence was supplied.
   - Regression-tested (`tests/test_score_aggregation.py`, `tests/test_pipeline_mudra_integration.py`) with the exact scenario the bug produces: a wrong identification whose predicted mudra's own rule still checks out perfectly clean -- rule-agreement scores 100, identification-accuracy correctly scores 0, proving the two are no longer conflated.

   **Second real bug found and fixed, same external review: missing-feature imputation used the wrong mean.** `mudra_classifier.predict_mudra()` filled a missing input feature with `row.mean()` -- the mean of that SAME ROW's other, unrelated features (a thumb-to-pinky distance averaged together with a 0/1 extended-finger flag, for instance). That's a semantically meaningless fill value, and it didn't even match how training handled missing values (`train_and_evaluate()` already used the training set's real per-feature mean). Fixed by saving those per-feature training means into the model bundle (`feature_means`) and using them at inference time too, so a missing feature gets filled with its own actual typical value, not an average of unrelated measurements. A bundle saved before this fix (missing the `feature_means` key) now fails loudly with a clear "retrain" message instead of silently falling back to the old wrong behavior. Regression-tested with a stub model that exposes the exact row handed to the classifier (`tests/test_mudra_classifier.py`) -- deliberately not testing this against the real RandomForest, since a real classifier's sensitivity to one feature isn't guaranteed to be observable and would make the test flaky; the stub proves the correct value is used, deterministically.

5. **Rhythm & beat detection.** Find the music's beat grid, and its repeating rhythmic cycle (the taal), from the audio track. **Beat grid: built** (`beat_sync_check.py`, via `librosa.beat.beat_track`) -- but until now this was a flat, uniform sequence of beat timestamps with no cycle structure, so feedback could only ever reference "the beat grid," never "sam." Raised directly as a real gap: in Kathak, "your chakkar was 1 beat off" is only a meaningful sentence relative to sam, the first/resolving beat of the taal cycle, and there are many taals (Teentaal, Jhaptaal, Ektaal, Rupak, and others), each with a different cycle length and internal structure.

   **Taal structure: built.** `taal_reference.py` has verified definitions (matra count, vibhag/subdivision breakdown, which vibhags are tali/clapped vs. khali/waved) for 12 taals relevant to Hindustani classical music and Kathak specifically -- sourced from a cited reference (Wikipedia's Vibhag article, cross-checked against several other sources), not transcribed from memory, since a wrong matra count would be a silent, hard-to-notice error baked into every downstream calculation. `build_cycle_map()`/`beats_from_sam()` take a beat grid + a taal + one known sam timestamp and compute, for any moment, which cycle/matra/vibhag it falls in and its signed distance in beats from the nearest sam -- the actual number "1 beat off sam" needs. Validated with synthetic tests (`tests/test_taal_reference.py`): every taal's vibhags sum to its matra count (data-entry sanity check), Teentaal's known real tali/khali positions (khali on matra 9) and Rupak's unusual khali-starting sam both check out, and the cycle math is verified against synthetic beat grids with a known ground truth.

   **Explicitly not built, and not guessed at:** automatically detecting *where* the first sam actually falls in a real recording, or which taal is being used. Both are genuinely hard, open audio-analysis problems (meter/downbeat tracking), and there's no labeled real audio in this project to validate a detector against -- attempting one now and shipping it unvalidated would repeat the exact mistake made earlier with the mudra classifier (building something that looks plausible but was never checked against reality). For now this needs the taal name and one sam timestamp supplied, not inferred.

   **Now wired into the dashboard.** The Overview upload form has an optional "Taal / sam" section (collapsed by default, so it doesn't get in the way when you don't have this info yet) -- a taal dropdown populated directly from `taal_reference.TAAL_DEFINITIONS` (so it can't drift out of sync with the reference data) and a plain seconds field for the first sam. `app/pipeline.py`'s `run_taal_analysis()` reuses the same beat grid `run_timing_analysis` already extracts (refactored into a shared `_extract_beat_grid()` so audio isn't decoded twice), matches it against each detected chakkar's own ending time via `beats_from_sam()`, and flags any landing 0.15+ beats off sam -- e.g. *"your chakkar ended about 1.30 beats after sam, not landing clean on the cycle."* The Chakkar Analysis tab shows this per chakkar alongside the existing count/orientation/stop-quality numbers. The 0.15-beat flagging threshold is a reasoned deadzone (same spirit as chakkar's own count-gap threshold), not calibrated against real taal-labeled audio -- none exists yet to calibrate against. Both the audio-free core (`_taal_flags_from_chakkar`, matching pattern in the sam-distance math) and the full wiring are tested (`tests/test_pipeline_taal.py`, 7 tests) and confirmed end to end against a real video (correctly returns nothing when a clip has no real audio signal, rather than faking a result).

6. **Movement timing check.** Compare movement timing (from step 2) and the piece's ending time against step 5's beat grid → *"your movements were not on beat,"* *"you ended 1 second before the ending beat."* **Built, both halves.** The "movements not on beat" half was already covered by `run_timing_analysis`'s windowed sync check (`flags_from_beat_sync`). The "ending time" half was the actual gap: `beat_sync_check.last_beat_offset_sec()` computes the signed distance in seconds between the clip's duration and the last beat in the detected grid, and `pipeline._flags_from_ending()` flags anything 0.5+ seconds off in either direction -- e.g. *"you ended about 1.20 seconds before the ending beat."* 0.5s is a reasoned deadzone (same spirit as the chakkar/taal thresholds), not calibrated against real labeled endings -- none exist in this project yet. Wired into `run_timing_analysis()`, reusing the beat grid already extracted for the windowed check (no extra audio decoding). Tested with synthetic beat grids (`tests/test_beat_sync_check.py`, `tests/test_pipeline_timing.py`).

7. **Tatkaar analysis.** Compare foot-strike timestamps (from step 2's foot data, or audio onset detection) against step 5's beat grid → *"your tatkaar was off-beat."* **Not built. Blocked on actually having tatkaar footage — none recorded yet.**

8. **Report assembly.** Collect every flag from steps 3/4/6/7, sort by timestamp, output the plain-English list. **Built.** `report_assembly.py` does no analysis of its own — it only formats what `chakkar_scoring.py`, `beat_sync_check.py`, and `mudra_reference.py` already produced (`flags_from_chakkar`, `flags_from_beat_sync`, `flags_from_mudra_checks`), merges them by timestamp, and `assemble_report()` renders each as one plain-English line, e.g. *"At 0:42, your chakkar did not return to your starting orientation..."* — matching this doc's own target example above verbatim. Rasa flags are deliberately excluded (see step 9 — unvalidated, kept out of the report). Wired into `analyze_video()` and shown on the dashboard's Reports tab.

9. **Abhinaya/rasa (facial expression) analysis.** Raised directly, with a supplied reference table of the 9 navarasas (Natya Shastra's classical emotions — Shringara/Love, Hasya/Joy, Karuna/Compassion, Raudra/Anger, Vira/Heroism, Bhayanaka/Fear, Bibhatsa/Disgust, Adbhuta/Wonder, Shanta/Peace) and each one's typical facial-expression description. **Reference logic built, validated on synthetic data, spot-checked against real footage, now wired into the dashboard -- deliberately labeled UNVALIDATED, same staged approach as mudra/taal before their real-data passes.**

   **Extraction.** `extract_landmarks.py` now runs a third MediaPipe detector alongside pose and hand — `FaceLandmarker`, with `output_face_blendshapes=True` — producing 52 named, semantically meaningful facial-muscle-activation scores per frame (`mouthSmileLeft`, `browDownLeft`, `eyeWideRight`, `jawOpen`, `noseSneerLeft`, etc.), much closer to the reference table's own language ("soft smile," "eyebrows drawn together," "wrinkled nose") than raw face landmark coordinates would be. The model file was verified real before downloading (`curl -sI` confirmed HTTP 200) rather than assumed from a URL. Confirmed working end-to-end: 100% face-detection rate across all 1854 frames of `mudra_01.mov`, and correctly 0% on `chakkar_01.mov` (the face isn't in frame during a spin — the right honest answer, not a bug).

   **Reference mapping.** `rasa_reference.py` translates each of the 9 rasas into specific blendshape criteria (each scored "high," "low," or "mid" against reasoned cutoffs, `HIGH_THRESHOLD = 0.4` / `LOW_THRESHOLD = 0.15`), using the same `check_X()`/`classify_X()` architecture already proven out in `mudra_reference.py`. Each rasa carries an honest confidence rating rather than presenting all nine as equally reliable: most are "high" or "medium" confidence, matched to blendshapes with a real anatomical connection to the description (Raudra's `browDown`+`eyeWide`, Bibhatsa's `noseSneer`, Adbhuta's `browOuterUp`+`eyeWide`+`jawOpen`). Vira (heroism) is marked **"low" confidence** on purpose — "steady gaze, lifted chin, composed face" describes a quality sustained over time and posture, not a facial-muscle movement a single video frame can show, so its definition here is really "no other rasa is showing" rather than a positive signature.

   **Same honest gap as mudra/taal before their real-data passes:** the threshold values are reasoned defaults, not calibrated against real labeled rasa footage — none exists in this project yet, since no piece has been recorded performing deliberate abhinaya.

   Validated with synthetic tests (`tests/test_rasa_reference.py`, 26 tests): every rasa's own criteria produce zero mismatches against a matching synthetic example, breaking one criterion is correctly flagged, missing blendshape data is correctly *not* treated as a mismatch (absence of data isn't evidence against a rasa), the "mid" band's boundaries are exact, and `classify_rasa()` reliably finds its own zero-mismatch match.

   **Real spot-check against `mudra_01.mov`** (a concentrating-but-not-performing-emotion face — a mudra demo, not a deliberate rasa piece): sampled 11 frames spread across the full clip, and `classify_rasa()` consistently picked **Vira** (0-1 mismatches each time), with **Karuna** as the closest runner-up throughout. This is the expected, honest result, not a failure — it confirms Vira correctly acts as the "nothing dramatic is happening" fallback for a neutral face. It also surfaced a real, worth-knowing pattern for later: a neutral/concentrating expression (e.g. during footwork or mudra practice, not actual abhinaya) may get misread as Karuna in a forced best-match, since a slight lack of smile during concentration overlaps with Karuna's "lips not smiling" criterion.

   **Now wired into the dashboard, deliberately unvalidated.** `pipeline.run_rasa_analysis()` samples the clip in fixed 1-second windows (no "expression is stable now" detector exists for faces the way `find_held_windows` detects held hand poses for mudra, so this samples on a plain clock instead), averages each window's blendshapes to smooth single-frame noise, drops windows where the face wasn't detected at all (rather than forcing a classification on no data), and runs `classify_rasa()` on what's left. Unlike mudra/taal, this does NOT feed into `overall_score` or the timestamped flag report — it's shown on its own "Abhinaya (Rasa)" tab, tagged with an "unvalidated" badge, and the tab's own note repeats the caveat in plain language, so an unvalidated rule-based guess never gets presented with the same implied confidence as chakkar/mudra/timing checks that *have* been checked against real data. This is intentionally further along the trust ladder than mudra/taal were at this stage: they waited for a real accuracy check before wiring in at all; rasa is wired in now, explicitly labeled, with the plan to validate against real navarasa footage once it's recorded and tighten or replace the current guesses at that point — not before. Tested end-to-end with a synthetic-blendshape integration test (`tests/test_pipeline_rasa_integration.py`) and confirmed against real data: run against `mudra_01.mov`'s real landmarks, it returns 62 one-second readings with no crashes, mostly classifying Vira (58/62) with a few Shanta (4/62) — consistent with the earlier spot-check finding that a neutral/concentrating face reads as Vira, not a new result.

**Explicitly separate, not part of this pipeline:** comparing metrics across multiple different dancers, and training an ML model on that comparison. Only relevant if a multi-dancer dataset gets built later — never required to analyze one uploaded video.

### App layer (dashboard)

An AI-generated mockup was used as the target reference for the app's shape: a sidebar-tabbed dashboard, not a plain text report. Each tab pulls from specific pipeline steps above:

- **Overview** — aggregate scores per category (e.g. "Mudra Accuracy: 92%", "Chakkar Count: 7", "Overall Score: 87%"). **Built.** `score_aggregation.py` turns each category's flags into a 0-100 score — `mudra_rule_agreement_score()`/`mudra_identification_accuracy_score()` (see step 4's circularity fix for why there are two, not one), `chakkar_quality_score()` (count/orientation/stop-quality, using the same flagging thresholds as `report_assembly.py` so a "clean-looking" performance doesn't lose points to sub-perceptible noise), `timing_accuracy_score()` (% of clip duration not covered by an unsynced window, clamped to [0, 100] -- see the real bug below), and `overall_score()` (equal-weighted average of whichever category scores are available). Some of the internal scaling (e.g. how fast points come off past the flagging threshold, and equal-weighting in `overall_score`) is a reasoned default, not calibrated against real "bad performance" ground truth — none exists yet. Rasa is deliberately excluded from `overall_score` (see step 9 — unvalidated).

  **Real bug found and fixed, from a full-codebase audit: `timing_accuracy_score()` could return a negative "accuracy."** It computed `100 * (1 - unsynced_duration / clip_duration_sec)` with no floor, and nothing guaranteed `unsynced_duration` stayed within `clip_duration_sec` -- `beat_sync_check.windowed_sync_check()` allows a window to end up to 1e-9s past the clip boundary (its own floating-point tolerance), and the function trusts whatever `windowed_results`/`clip_duration_sec` it's handed rather than cross-checking one against the other, so a caller passing a shorter duration than the one the windows were actually computed against could produce a much more negative score. Fixed by clamping the result to `[0, 100]`. Regression-tested (`tests/test_score_aggregation.py`) with the exact failure case -- an unsynced window longer than the given clip duration -- confirming it now floors at 0 instead of going negative, plus a check that a fully-synced clip still caps at exactly 100.
- **Pose View** — raw skeleton overlay drawn on the video. Already built (used for pilot QA overlays), just needs exposing in the app.
- **Mudra Analysis / Chakkar Analysis tabs** — pull directly from pipeline steps 4 / 3.
- **Abhinaya (Rasa)** — pulls from step 9. Built, wired in, explicitly labeled "unvalidated" (badge in the tab list and a caveat in the tab itself) since the underlying thresholds haven't been checked against real deliberate-abhinaya footage yet.
- **Tatkaar (rhythmic footwork detection)** — Step 7. **Built**, audio-based rather than pose-based: `run_tatkaar_analysis()` (`app/pipeline.py`) reuses `comparison.find_dense_stretches()` unmodified, calling it on one video's own onsets instead of comparing two tracks. A tatkaar phrase is a sustained run of closely-spaced percussive onsets (foot/ghungroo strikes) -- exactly what that function was already built to detect for teacher-vs-student comparison. Chosen over a pose-based "foot lifts then lands" heuristic: tatkaar's whole point is producing an audible strike sound, monocular RGB pose data can't tell a real ground-contact strike apart from any other quick foot movement (a chakkar prep step, a weight shift) without corroborating sound anyway, and the one video this project had for pose work happened to crop the feet out of frame entirely (see the chakkar pilot notes below) -- audio doesn't care about camera framing. The dashboard's `#tab-tatkaar` (previously mislabeled -- it showed step 6's arm/beat-timing output instead, with no real footwork content behind it) now shows this instead; that arm/beat-timing check got its own honestly-named "Timing" tab.

  **UNVALIDATED against real tatkaar footage** -- `find_dense_stretches()`'s thresholds (`DENSE_GAP_THRESHOLD_SEC`, `MIN_DENSE_STRETCH_ONSETS`) are reasoned defaults, never checked against a real recording (none exists in this project yet). Confirmed working end-to-end against synthetic audio (a 10-second clip with a 12-click burst spaced 0.15s apart correctly detected as one dense stretch, 12 strikes, ~7.3/sec) -- this proves the wiring (ffmpeg audio extraction -> onset detection -> dense-stretch grouping -> event packaging) is correct, not that the thresholds match real tatkaar. Also inherits `comparison.py`'s own honest limitation: an onset can't be told apart from a clap or any other percussive sound, only counted. `tests/test_pipeline_tatkaar.py` covers the wrapper logic (None-handling, event packaging) with synthetic onset arrays; the underlying `find_dense_stretches()` grouping logic itself is already covered by `test_comparison.py`.
- **Comparison** — **Built.** Teacher-vs-student mode: user uploads two videos (a teacher's performance + their own take on the same piece), scripts/comparison.py aligns and compares them, and the `/compare` route + Comparison tab return an action-by-action breakdown against the teacher's video as ground truth. The two pieces the original scoping called for:
  1. **Alignment** — the two performances won't be frame-synced (different start, different pacing). `fastdtw` (sitting unused in requirements.txt since the original project setup) runs over both tracks' `librosa.onset.onset_strength` envelopes to map any moment in the teacher's timeline to the corresponding moment in the student's. **Real bug caught and fixed during testing**: plain amplitude-distance DTW is ambiguous across the long near-silent stretches between onsets (near-zero cost no matter how the flat regions get matched), so the raw approach let the path drift arbitrarily within a quiet stretch and still call it "optimal" — confirmed directly with a synthetic tempo-shifted test where a teacher onset mapped to a completely different action's region. Fixed by adding a small time-position term to each frame's feature vector so ties during silence resolve toward a proportionally-similar-in-time alignment, without overriding a real envelope match when the audio content actually disagrees.
  2. **Event detection** — audio onsets (`librosa.onset.onset_detect`) stand in for discrete events like claps and stomps, since both are audible. This can't tell a clap apart from a stomp or a bell hit — that would need a dedicated sound classifier, which doesn't exist — so flags describe them honestly as "movement sound(s)" rather than claiming to know the specific type. Onsets close together (within 0.6s) are grouped into one "action" (e.g. two quick claps) before comparing counts, so a burst of onsets isn't scored one-by-one against a possibly-misaligned single student onset.

  Validated with a synthetic regression suite (tests/test_comparison.py): identical performances score 100% match with no flags, a missing/extra onset in the student's track is correctly flagged with the right counts, and a uniformly tempo-shifted student performance (1.25x slower throughout) still aligns and matches correctly after the DTW fix above.

  **Real gap found and fixed: dense rhythmic stretches (tatkaar).** The above (event detection + DTW alignment) works for occasional, well-separated gestures -- a clap here, a stomp there -- but not for tatkaar. A tatkaar phrase is many bols in a row at roughly the same fast pace, so cluster_onsets() collapsed an entire avartan into one giant "action," and if the student did an extra avartan the whole thing turned into a single garbled, meaningless flag (confirmed directly with a synthetic 1-avartan-vs-2-avartan test: it produced one nonsense "teacher had 16, you had 31" flag instead of anything useful). Worse, feeding that length mismatch into fastdtw distorted the alignment for other events later in the piece too.

  Fixed by treating dense rhythmic stretches as their own category, found and handled *before* the general action-matching logic runs:
  - `find_dense_stretches()` groups a sustained run of closely-spaced onsets (8+ onsets, each within 0.7s of the last) into a "dense stretch" -- separately from cluster_onsets()'s short-burst grouping, which still handles quick multi-claps.
  - Dense stretches are compared by onset **count**, not matched bol-by-bol (which DTW fundamentally can't do for a repeated cycle -- it has no concept of "the student did this whole section twice," only of stretching/compressing time). A count mismatch that's close to a whole-number ratio gets phrased as a likely extra repetition, e.g. *"the teacher did 16 strikes and you did 32 -- looks like about 2x the teacher's, possibly 1 extra repetition."*
  - Dense-stretch time ranges are removed from the envelope entirely before it's handed to fastdtw, so a length mismatch inside a tatkaar section can't warp the mapping of anything before or after it. Confirmed directly: claps placed after a 1-vs-2-avartan mismatch mapped to within 0 seconds of their true time post-fix, vs. drifting several seconds off before the fix.
  - If one side has a dense stretch with no counterpart at all (not just a different count -- e.g. the student has a tatkaar section the teacher's video doesn't have anywhere), it's reported as an unmatched extra rather than force-paired with something unrelated.

  **Honest limitation, not yet solved**: the 0.7s/8-onset thresholds are reasoned defaults, not calibrated against real tatkaar audio -- no tatkaar footage has been recorded for this project yet (project_description.md's Experiment 3, the tatkaar audio-onset pilot, hasn't been started). The logic itself is validated with synthetic data (tests/test_comparison.py), but the exact cutoffs may need adjusting once real recordings exist. Also, this still can't identify actual avartan/taal-cycle boundaries (that would need knowing the taal, e.g. teentaal's 16-beat cycle, and detecting the *sam*) -- it only reports relative strike counts, which is honest but coarser than "you did exactly one extra avartan."
- **Reports** — the detailed timestamped critique, pipeline step 8's output. **Built.**
- **Live capture** — perform in front of a webcam and get feedback while recording, instead of only after uploading a finished video. **Built**, additive on top of the batch pipeline above, not a rewrite of it: `app/live_session.py` holds a live session's persistent MediaPipe landmarkers (created once per session, not per chunk -- reloading the `.task` model bundles every ~1.5s would be its own latency bug) and rolling landmarks/audio buffers; `app/live_pipeline.py` decodes each recorded chunk, extends those buffers, and re-runs `run_chakkar_analysis`/`run_mudra_analysis`/`run_rasa_analysis`/`run_timing_analysis`/`run_taal_analysis` -- unmodified -- against everything accumulated so far. The browser (`app/static/app.js`) records ~1.5s chunks by cycling `MediaRecorder.start()`/`stop()` rather than using one recorder with `timeslice`: a single recorder's later chunks are headerless Matroska continuations nothing but that same recorder can decode, while each `stop()` finalizes an independently-decodable file -- confirmed directly against real phone-recorded chunks before building the rest (all decoded cleanly; `cv2`'s own reported fps/frame-count metadata for these short chunks was unreliable and had to be derived from frames-actually-read instead). New Flask routes (`/live/start`, `/live/chunk`, `/live/stop`) return a snapshot shaped exactly like `analyze_video()`'s return value, so the existing `renderResults()` is reused unmodified for live updates -- no separate live-mode rendering code exists.

  **The one real correctness problem live mode introduces, and how it's handled**: `segment_rotation_bursts` (chakkar) and `find_held_windows` (mudra) both close out whatever's still in progress at the end of whatever data they're given -- re-running them every ~1.5s on a growing buffer means a spin or a mudra hold still actively happening right now would otherwise get reported as "finished," then "un-finish and re-finish longer" next tick. Fixed with a finalize margin: an event is only forwarded to the client once it's safely behind the current buffer's edge (chakkar: `SEGMENT_MERGE_GAP_SEC + SEGMENT_SCORE_PAD_SEC`, mudra: `STILL_WINDOW_SEC` -- reusing those modules' own tuning constants, not new guessed numbers), holding anything more recent as pending and re-checking it next tick. Rasa needed the same care for a different reason: it skips a window entirely (not just marks it) when no face is detected in it, which would silently shift list positions across ticks if events were matched by index instead of by `start_sec` -- confirmed directly when a real test session's face left frame after ~1.5s and window-position-based matching stopped finalizing new (correctly detected, earlier) windows.

  Two audio-dependent checks (timing, taal) are throttled to roughly every 4s of wall time rather than every chunk -- tempo/beat detection over the whole rolling buffer is the one part of this expensive enough to matter -- and, like the batch pipeline, report nothing until enough real audio signal has accumulated (`MIN_DURATION_FOR_WINDOWED_CHECK`/`MIN_AUDIO_RMS`), not a live-mode-specific gap.

  Verified end-to-end multiple ways, not just unit-tested: directly in Python against real phone-recorded chunks (buffers grew correctly, facial-expression readings finalized and correctly stopped once the face left frame, nothing crashed across ~9s), through an actual browser via Playwright + Chrome's fake-camera-device flags (Go Live → camera permission → chunk recording/upload → live-updating dashboard tabs → Stop, zero console or server errors), and with a full-length synthetic stress test (a real `LiveSession` driven through 1200 chunks of synthetic ffmpeg-generated video/audio, ~1.5s each, to the full `MAX_SESSION_DURATION_SEC` cap): tick-processing time grew from ~0.30s to ~0.53s average over the 30 minutes (roughly linear in accumulated buffer size, matching the deliberate "reprocess everything every tick" design -- not a runaway/exponential cost), the periodic throttled timing/taal recompute tick grew from ~1.6s to ~2.3s (occasionally exceeding the ~1.5s real-time chunk interval right at the 30-minute mark, meaning a real session's very last few recording cycles could very occasionally run under a second longer than usual -- not a freeze or visible backlog), memory grew ~238MB over the full session in line with the expected size of the rolling raw-audio buffer at that duration (no leak signature), and zero exceptions occurred across all 1200 chunks. New tests: `tests/test_extract_landmarks_refactor.py`, `tests/test_pipeline_beat_grid_split.py`, `tests/test_live_session.py`, `tests/test_live_pipeline.py`, `tests/test_server_live_routes.py` -- pure-logic/fake-object tests for the bookkeeping above, not re-testing the underlying chakkar/mudra/rasa/timing logic itself (already covered by their own existing tests).

  **Honest gap**: found and fixed mid-build, not left as a landmine -- calling `run_mudra_analysis` live surfaced that `data/mudra_training/model.joblib` predated a recent `predict_mudra()` fix requiring a `feature_means` key, and raised on every single prediction (breaking mudra identification in the upload flow too, not just live -- a real regression, not a live-mode-specific issue). Retrained against the same dataset (`dataset_combined_full.csv`) the shipped model was originally trained on. A live-mode-specific defensive layer stayed regardless: one bad prediction now degrades to "mudra unavailable for this tick" instead of taking down the whole live snapshot, since a multi-minute live session shouldn't hard-fail on a single unlucky frame the way one batch `/analyze` call reasonably can. See step 4's `MUDRA_MIN_CONFIDENCE` note for the retrain's real-world-accuracy re-verification status (spot-checked, not yet rigorously re-verified against the real recording order).

**Backend housekeeping, built.** From an earlier honest audit of the dashboard: `app/uploads/` grew forever (every `/analyze` and `/compare` call wrote a permanent session folder, nothing ever removed one), both routes leaked raw Python exception text to the browser on failure, no upload validation existed, and `/compare` was missing the same filename-sanitization fix `/analyze` already had. Fixed: session folders older than 24h are swept automatically at server startup and on each request (can't delete immediately -- the browser fetches the overlay/original video from `/media/<session_id>/...` after the response returns, so a folder has to survive at least that long); both routes now log the real exception server-side and return a generic message to the client instead of leaking internals; uploads are restricted to a small set of real video extensions and a 1GB total request-size cap; `/compare`'s teacher/student filenames now go through `secure_filename` too.

**Real security bug found and fixed, from a full-codebase audit: `/media/<session_id>/<filename>` was a path-traversal vulnerability, not just a theoretical risk.** `session_id` came straight from the URL into `send_from_directory()`'s *directory* argument -- Flask's own documentation explicitly warns this is unsafe, since `send_from_directory` only sanitizes the `filename` argument against traversal, not the directory. Confirmed by actually exploiting it before fixing anything: `GET /media/../server.py` returned this project's own server source code. Fixed by validating `session_id` against the one shape this app ever generates (`uuid.uuid4().hex[:12]`, a 12-character lowercase hex string) before it touches the filesystem at all -- anything else (`..`, `/`, URL-encoded variants) gets rejected with a plain 404 before reaching `send_from_directory`. Regression-tested directly against the exploit (`tests/test_server.py`): the literal attack, a URL-encoded variant, and a non-hex session id are all confirmed blocked, while a well-formed-but-nonexistent session id still gets an ordinary missing-file 404 rather than being wrongly rejected.

**Second full-codebase audit (after live capture + tatkaar detection landed), real bugs found and fixed:**
- **XSS via the "expected mudra sequence" field.** `app.js` inserted the user-supplied `expected_mudra` text straight into `innerHTML` unescaped; the server only lowercases/strips this field by design (a whitelist would create false rejections for legitimate mudra names), so any HTML/script typed into that textarea would execute when results render. Fixed with a small `escapeHtml()` helper applied at the injection point. Self-XSS only today (single-user tool), but a real gap.
- **Live-session resource leaks on two error paths.** If `getUserMedia` failed *after* `/live/start` had already created a server-side session (loaded landmarkers + mudra model), the client only cleared its local variable and never told the server to clean up -- the session sat alive until the 5-minute idle sweep. Separately, double-clicking "Go Live" before the first click's `await`s resolved could fire two full session-creation + camera-grant sequences, leaking the first camera stream and orphaning its session. Fixed: the failure path now calls `/live/stop` for the orphaned session, and a `liveStarting` guard makes a second click before the first resolves a no-op.
- **Stale live results could overwrite the final "stopped" snapshot.** The recording loop only checked whether the session was still running right after recording a chunk, not after the subsequent `/live/chunk` network round-trip -- if Stop was clicked while that request was in flight, its response could still land and re-render over the real final state. Fixed with a second running-check after the fetch resolves.
- **Overview tab's "Tatkaar Tempo" card was mislabeled by the tab-split above.** It displays the Movement Analysis (arm-vs-beat) check's tempo reading, which has no connection to the real Tatkaar Analysis feature (onset-count based, no tempo concept at all) -- reintroducing the exact kind of mislabeling this pass fixed elsewhere. Renamed to plain "Tempo."
- **A live session could be closed by `/live/stop` or the idle sweep while a `/live/chunk` request for that same session was already queued waiting on its lock** -- the queued request held a stale reference to already-closed landmarkers and a deleted work directory. Not reachable under the current single-threaded dev server, but a real gap in the locking design that would surface under any real concurrency (e.g. a threaded/multi-worker deployment, a realistic next step given this app's LAN-access history). Hardened: `/live/chunk` re-checks the session is still live *after* acquiring its lock, and the idle sweep now closes a session under that same lock instead of unlocked.
- **Client-supplied `duration_sec` on `/live/chunk` was trusted with only a "does it parse as a float" check.** An implausible value (clock drift on a throttled/backgrounded mobile tab, or a buggy client) silently corrupts `live_pipeline`'s effective-fps math and the finalize-margin invariant chakkar/mudra event reporting depends on -- inflating or stalling `session.duration_sec` independent of the real video. Bounded to `(0, 10]` seconds (chunks are ~1.5s by design; 10s is a generous ceiling, not the expected value).
- **Batch `/analyze` extracted audio and ran tempo/beat detection twice** (once each for timing and taal analysis) whenever both taal name and sam time were supplied, contradicting `_extract_beat_grid`'s own docstring describing it as shared work -- the live-capture path already caches and shares one beat grid across both checks, but the batch path never received the same treatment. Fixed by extracting the beat grid once in `analyze_video()` and passing it to both. Not a correctness bug (identical results either way), just a 100%-reproducible doubling of audio-processing latency. Confirmed the fix with a real synthetic-video run: exactly one `_extract_beat_grid` call regardless of whether the grid ends up usable.
- **A whole-clip tatkaar/dense-rhythmic performance crashed the Comparison feature.** `align_envelopes()`'s `np.max()` call raises on an empty array, and `compare_performances()` can hand it exactly that -- a clip that's entirely one sustained dense stretch (a realistic footwork-drill practice video) has every envelope sample removed before alignment. Fixed to return an empty path gracefully in that case (the caller's action-comparison loop never dereferences it when there's nothing sparse to compare, confirmed with a regression test).
- **Untracked hand landmarks were silently counted as "finger curled" instead of staying missing**, in `extract_features.py`'s `_extended` columns (`pip_angle > 160` evaluates a NaN pip_angle to plain `False`, not NaN). Realistic trigger: a hand briefly losing tracking (motion blur, occlusion) during part of a held mudra window. `run_mudra_analysis`'s majority-vote averaging over a window then silently votes "curled" for untracked frames instead of excluding them, which can flip a technically-correct mudra to a false "should be extended but was curled" mismatch. Fixed with `np.where` to keep untracked frames as real NaN, matching how the other (continuous) hand-feature columns already behave, and skip correctly under pandas' `.mean()`.

**Flagged, not fixed -- needs a real-world-filming-convention answer, not a guess:** MediaPipe's hand handedness classification assumes a mirrored/selfie-style input image. Nothing in `extract_landmarks.py` or the training-data-building scripts corrects for this. If this project's actual videos (training photos and user uploads alike) are filmed normally (camera facing the dancer, not a front-facing selfie camera), every mudra event's reported `hand_side` is the *opposite* of the dancer's real hand -- classification accuracy itself likely survives this (training and inference use the same convention consistently), but any user-facing text naming a hand would be wrong for every single event. Not fixed here because flipping the label without knowing the actual filming convention used for existing training data risks trading a consistent-but-mislabeled system for an inconsistent one.

## Chakkar Pilot Test (Experiment 2 — can we count spins?)

1. Got the actual video files onto disk. You'd said "downloaded videos" but that's a browser action — nothing lands in a project folder automatically. I found them in your Downloads folder and copied them into dance-project/data/raw/ so there'd be stable file paths to work with.

2. Looked at the raw video first, before running any algorithm. I pulled a few still frames out of each clip (first/middle/last) and looked at them directly — checking framing (is the whole body visible? is the camera angle sane? is it too dark/blurry?). This matters because if the video itself is bad (e.g., feet cut off, motion blur), any failure later could be a recording problem, not an algorithm problem. Found: camera was angled up and cropped your feet out — fine for spin-counting (which only needs shoulders), but would sink a footwork test later.

3. Ran a pose-detection model on every single frame of both videos. This is MediaPipe Pose — it's a pretrained model that looks at each video frame and tries to output the pixel location of ~33 body joints (shoulders, hips, knees, etc.), each with a confidence/"visibility" score. This is the same kind of model used for things like Snapchat filters or fitness apps — it doesn't know anything about Kathak, it just finds body joints.

4. Test A — "does tracking even survive the movement?" For every frame, I checked: did the model find a person at all, and specifically did it find both shoulders with high confidence? I counted what fraction of frames passed. Result: 100% of frames in both clips had a person detected, and both shoulders visible. That's the first pass/fail gate — if this were low (say 60%), there'd be no point trying to count anything, because the raw tracking would already be unreliable.

5. Test B — "can we count the spins from that tracking data?" For each frame, I drew a line between your left and right shoulder and computed the angle that line makes (basic trig — atan2, the same math as finding the angle of a slope). As you spin, that angle changes frame by frame. Problem: raw angle math wraps around at 180°/-180° (like a compass, going from 179° to -179° is actually just a 2° step, not a 358° jump) — so I "unwrapped" it, meaning I let the angle keep climbing past 180°, 270°, 360°, etc. instead of snapping back. Then: total degrees rotated ÷ 360 = number of full spins.

6. Checked the algorithm's count against what you actually did. You told me video 1 = 1 spin, video 2 = 3 spins — that's the ground truth, the "correct answer" a human knows and the algorithm doesn't. Algorithm output: video 1 ≈ 0.99 spins (rounds to 1), video 2 ≈ 3.00 spins (rounds to 3). Both matched exactly.

7. Tested a longer clip to check for "drift." Two clips (1 spin, 3 spins) is a very small test — if the angle math has a small per-frame error, it might not show up over 1-3 spins but could snowball into a visibly wrong count over more rotations. So you recorded a third clip with 10 continuous fast spins, and I ran the same process on it. Result: 10.02 estimated vs. 10 true — still rounds to exactly 10, and critically, the error didn't grow with more rotations. I also looked directly at the plotted angle data (a chart of the raw angle and the "unwrapped" angle over time) to confirm the climb was smooth and continuous the whole way through, not just a lucky final number.

## Movement Pilot Test (Experiment 1 — does pose tracking survive ordinary movement?)

This is a different question from the chakkar test. Chakkars are the hardest case (fast spinning). This experiment asks a more basic question first: does pose tracking even work reasonably well on *normal* Kathak-ish movement — arm gestures, walking, posture — not just spins? And does it hold up the same way across two different, independently-built tracking models, or does it just look good because of a quirk in one specific model?

1. Got the video onto disk, same as before — found it in Downloads, copied it into dance-project/data/raw/movement/.

2. Ran two different pose-tracking models on every frame, side by side, instead of just one:
   - **MediaPipe Pose** — the same model used for the chakkar test. Finds ~33 body points, including some face detail.
   - **MoveNet** (specifically the "Thunder" version, from Google) — a different, independently-trained model. Finds 17 body points (no face detail, just the main joints: shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, ankles).

   The reason to run two models instead of one: MediaPipe and MoveNet were built by different teams using different training data and different internal designs. If a movement trips up *one* model, that could just be a quirk of that specific model. If a movement trips up *both* models, that's much stronger evidence the movement itself is genuinely hard to track (motion blur, limbs overlapping, etc. — not just one model's weakness). Agreement between two independent models is a much better signal than one model alone.

3. For each frame, recorded whether each model found a person, and how confident it was. MediaPipe reports a 0-1 "visibility" score per joint (I averaged across joints for a per-frame number). MoveNet reports its own 0-1 "score" per joint the same way. These two confidence numbers aren't on directly comparable scales (different models calibrate confidence differently), so they're read separately, not compared like-for-like.

4. Drew the skeleton (all the tracked joints, connected with lines) directly on top of the video for both models, and saved those as separate "overlay" videos — this is the same idea as the guide-lines apps like Snapchat draw on your face, just for the whole body. Having the skeleton drawn on the actual footage is what makes it possible to visually check "does this look right?" rather than just trusting a number.

5. Since manually watching every single frame of every video isn't practical, saved one still image every 15 frames (about 2 per second), with both models' overlays side by side in the same image, into a `review_stills` folder. This gives a manageable, evenly-spread sample to look through by eye.

6. Manually looked at a handful of those saved stills, spread across the whole clip, and rated each one:
   - **Good** — skeleton position looks correct.
   - **Usable** — small error, but you could still measure things from it.
   - **Failed** — wrong limb, lost the person, or a major jump/glitch.

   Checked: a plain standing pose, a crossed-arm gesture, a side-profile pose with a raised hand, and — deliberately picked because it looked like the blurriest one — a fast arm-raise with visible motion blur. All four were rated Good on both models. Even the blurry one tracked correctly, and MediaPipe's low-confidence (red) markers correctly lined up with the genuinely ambiguous points (like a hand foreshortened toward the camera) rather than being wrong everywhere.

7. Result so far: both models detected a person on 100% of frames in this clip, and the small manual spot-check found no failures. This is an early good sign for the "does tracking survive ordinary movement" question, but it's not the full test yet — only about 4 stills out of 52 saved ones have actually been eyeballed so far (the original plan calls for checking hundreds), and this was one mixed clip (arm movements + some walking together) rather than the separate clean clips (stance / arms / torso / walking / combined) the plan called for. No "harder" conditions (looser clothing, different camera angle, busier background) have been tried yet either.
